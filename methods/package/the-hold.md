# The Hold — why a stranger keeps watching

The retention doctrine of this Showrunner OS. The beat-sheet method
(`beat-sheet-prompt.md`) applies this file to every video you outline, and you should
read it once yourself: these four parts are how you judge any draft the system hands you.

The one law over all of it: the click carries a promise, and the video has to keep it.
YouTube reads what happens after the click more closely than anything else. Every part
below exists to keep that promise, minute after minute.

## Part 1 · The hook (the first 30 seconds)

The hook is the first 30 seconds, and its only job is to earn the next few minutes.
Most of the people who ever leave, leave here.

- Open on the problem or the stakes. Say the biggest, most unavoidable thing first, the
  thing the viewer clicked to resolve.
- Lead with your strongest moment. Never build up to it; the build-up is where they leave.
- Start with a continuation word: This, So, Here's. You can't follow those with a greeting.
- Kill on sight: "hey guys", "welcome back", "today we're going to talk about", any
  channel housekeeping. All of it signals nothing interesting is coming.

## Part 2 · The spine (sections and re-hooks)

Three to five sections, in a format that fits the concept: a ranked list, a how-I-did-it,
a why-this-fails, a this-versus-that. The shape gives the viewer a map; the re-hooks give
them a reason to stay on it.

- Open a loop early that only a later section closes, and never hold more than two loops
  open at once. Two build pull; a third builds anxiety, and they leave.
- Re-hook at every section break: touch the pain from the hook again, then promise that
  what's coming is stronger than what they just got. First re-hook by minute two or
  three; four or five across a video is plenty.
- Order your points second-best first, best second, so the open is strong and slot two
  delivers past it. The one exception is a ranked-list format, where you climb to the
  best last. Never open with your weakest point or with something they already know.

## Part 3 · The setups (the stake before each section)

Viewers leave at two places: the hook, and the setups, the lead-in lines before each
section. Everyone works the hook; almost nobody works the setups.

- Before each section, say what's coming and what it costs to skip it, tied to the
  reason they clicked in the first place.
- Drill the why until it's concrete: their goal, the problem in the way, what that
  problem costs them in real life. Stop only when you hit a specific scene, a number, or
  a consequence you can picture.
- Banned: hollow bridges. "Most people get this wrong", "nobody talks about this",
  "here's the thing" name no stake and signal low value. If you can't name the concrete
  payoff of the next section, the bridge is empty. Cut it.

## Part 4 · The payoff (and the close)

- Reality has to beat expectations. Deliver past what the setup promised; match it
  exactly and it reads as less.
- Drip-feed context. Hold information back on purpose so there's always a reason to
  stay; front-load everything and the middle goes hollow.
- Never wrap up. No recap, no "so those were the five signs", no "hope that was
  helpful". Wrap-up language tells the viewer the video is over, and they take the exit.
- End on the payoff, then make one ask, to one destination, the one in your
  brand-baseline's business connection. One.
